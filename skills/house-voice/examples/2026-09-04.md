## Alfred Schutz, "On Multiple Realities" (Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, 1945)

Source/Author: Alfred Schutz → the paper keeps its hardest idea light by
attaching each region of reality to a mundane anchor: the world of working
differs from the world of phantasy because of "a specific tension and a
specific epoché," and the tension is described as the accent of "thus and not
otherwise." A technical distinction rides on a phrase a reader could say
aloud. → Apply when a learning piece must define a fine gradation: give each
grade its own plain tag phrase and repeat the tag verbatim. → Do not apply
when the gradations are not genuinely distinct; repeated tags on a fuzzy
distinction read as incantation.

## Erving Goffman, "The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life" (1956/1959)

Source/Author: Erving Goffman → the dramaturgical argument never defines
"performance" abstractly; it opens with the Shetland hotel's dining room,
where the same room switches from one social definition at dinner to another
at the staff's own meal, and the definition of the concept is induced from
the scene. The example precedes and replaces the definition. → Apply when a
sociological concept risks sounding like jargon: spend the opening paragraphs
on one witnessed scene and let the term arrive only after the scene has done
its work. → Do not apply in data-heavy sections, where a scene delays the
numbers and reads as throat-clearing.

## Bethany McLean Fortune archive (Stanford mirror) — the two-cell title

Source/Author: Fortune, March 5, 2001 → the article's question mark is a
title that files its own burden of proof downward while the body carries
attributed jokes ("If you figure it out, let me know") as evidence; the
mechanism (title asks, sources testify) lets a national magazine attack a
$126 target without one assertion of its own. → Apply when reporting a
dispute: let the quoted evasions form the argument's spine. → Do not apply
where no on-record sources exist; a question title without quotes is a
shrug.

## Granovetter 1973 opening — the ordinary question as plot

Source/Author: Granovetter, AJS 78(6) → the paper's drama comes from a
question everyone has answered ("how did you find your last job?") treated
as data; the reader supplies the scene from their own life, so no invented
scene is needed. → Apply when the subject is universal behavior: open on
the shared experience, then show the survey numbers overturning it. → Do
not apply to specialist subjects, where the reader has no personal scene
to contribute.

## SEC administrative order, In re Knight Capital (Release No. 34-70694)

Source/Author: SEC Division of Enforcement, Oct 16, 2013 → the order's drama
is carried entirely by numbered paragraphs that each state one finding and stop:
"one of Knight's technicians did not copy the new code to one of eight SMARS
computer servers." No adverb grades the negligence; the sequence of paragraph
numbers is the timeline. → Apply to incident narratives: give each failure its
own short numbered or dated paragraph and let the reader feel the compounding.
→ Do not apply to argument sections, where numbered findings read as a
complaint.

## Zoom Form S-1 (March 2019) — the load-bearing sentence repeated at seams

Source/Author: Zoom S-1 → the filing repeats one sentence nearly verbatim in
five different sections: "55% of our 344 customers that contributed more than
$100,000 of revenue started with at least one free host." A reader entering the
document anywhere meets the key fact again. → Apply in long features: repeat
the single load-bearing statistic sentence at two or three section seams so
skimmers keep the spine. → Do not apply more than once per 800 words, and
never with soft numbers, or repetition reads as padding.
