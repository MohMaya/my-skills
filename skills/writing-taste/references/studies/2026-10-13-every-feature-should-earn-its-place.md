# Every feature should earn its place — Karri Saarinen

- Link: https://karrisaarinen.com/posts/every-feature-should-earn-its-place/
- Author: Karri Saarinen (@karrisaarinen), Linear CEO, April 12, 2026 (originally published on X)
- Genre: product and leadership writing
- Found via: already on the roster as Shiv's seed for product/craft writing; the phrase circulates as a product rule on X, and Product Picnic quoted the essay when arguing that AI makes feature creep cheaper, not wiser
- Studied: 2026-10-13

## Core claim

When the cost of building a feature falls, the cost that still bites is carrying it—maintenance, interactions, support, and what users must understand—so every feature must earn its place because its existence, not its implementation, is expensive.

## Thinking moves

- **Grant the fashionable premise, then rename the cost.** He opens by admitting execution is getting cheaper and that teams can explore more. The turn is not "cheap is bad"; it is that the bill moved from *building* to *carrying*. The reader who believed the new story still has to face the old bill under a new name.
- **Name the default he is breaking.** "Just build it and see" gets stated as a common story and as a tactic with real uses, before he says it is not free. The reader is not asked to abandon exploration; they are asked to stop pretending shipping is the expensive part.
- **Find the harder failure mode.** Failure that is obvious is easy: remove it and move on. The dangerous case is the feature that succeeds just enough to survive—some users, a few workflows, now owned forever. Naming that case is the essay's intellectual center; without it, "earn its place" would sound like taste talk.
- **Concede where the tactic works, with a boundary.** Exploration, prototypes, internal tools, temporary experiments, and isolated ideas are where cheap building is genuinely valuable. The claim stops at features that enter the product and shape it over time.
- **Update the rule for agents.** Agents make adding easier, so restraint becomes more important, not less. He restates the old engineering discipline (build was expensive, so you chose carefully) as a deliberate choice now that the force that enforced it is gone.
- **End by flipping the reason.** The title returns as the close, then one sentence changes the ground: not because implementation is expensive, but because existence is. The claim ends at its true size and its true cost.

## Writing moves

- **Opening:** a "common story" in one sentence the reader already hears in product meetings. No company lore, no metrics, no preamble.
- **Structure:** concede → rename the cost → list what a shipped feature adds → grant where cheap building helps → name the partial-success trap → agents raise the stakes → close on the title and the flipped reason. About 450 words; one idea, climbed once.
- **Transitions:** short declaratives that place the reader ("There is some truth to that."; "In a world where building is easy, the real cost comes later."). The argument needs no headings.
- **Rhythm:** the build-vs-carry turn is broken across a line so the second sentence lands alone: "But while the cost of building a feature is going down." / "The cost of carrying a feature is not." Elsewhere, plain sentences and one list of five consequences (interactions, failure modes, design decisions, support questions, expectations) that makes "surface area" concrete without a metaphor lecture.
- **Concrete examples:** not product screenshots—product mechanics. Surface area, workflows that depend on a half-success, the threshold for adding falling below the threshold for living with it. The reader supplies their own backlog.
- **What he cut:** no Linear metrics, no feature autopsy, no AI hype, no how-to checklist. The word "Agents" gets one paragraph. Everything else would dilute the renamed cost.
- **Ending:** the title as its own paragraph, then the one-line flip. Necessary, not ornamental.

## Excerpts

1. Opening as the shared story: "A common story in product development goes like this: execution is cheap now, so why not build more things and see what sticks?"
2. The cost renamed (broken across a line for punch): "But while the cost of building a feature is going down." / "The cost of carrying a feature is not."
3. Boundary where the tactic works: "It works well for exploration. It works for prototypes, internal tools, temporary experiments, and ideas that can remain isolated until they earn a place in the product."
4. The harder failure mode: "The harder case is a feature that succeeds just enough to survive."
5. Closing flip: "Not because implementation is expensive, but because its existence is."

## Exercise

Pick one feature you shipped in the last quarter (or one you are about to ship). Write two columns: cost to *build* it, and cost to *carry* it for a year (interactions it creates, failure modes, support questions, what users must learn, what you cannot delete without breaking a workflow). Circle any item that would still be true if implementation were free. Then write one sentence that says whether it has earned its place—and the boundary where "just build it and see" would still be the right move.
