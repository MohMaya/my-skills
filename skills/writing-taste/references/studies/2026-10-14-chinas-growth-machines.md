## Core claim

China's industrial rise is not a Beijing-directed command economy but a continent-sized tournament of local governments that act like venture capitalists—competing for investment, capturing growth's upside, and building clusters—so the West's lesson is to revive its own broken local growth machines, not copy central planning.

## Thinking moves

- **Replace the reader's frame before arguing the details.** The Western story attributes China's success to top-down picking of winners. The essay names that story, then swaps it for "rulemaker in a great tournament" before any firm case appears. Everything that follows is evidence for the swap, not a tour of Chinese industry that hopes the reader will notice the pattern.
- **Open with a concrete analogy the reader already feels.** "Imagine if Y Combinator owned and ran San Francisco" turns municipal government into a familiar VC portfolio. The reader who knows YC already understands career incentives, portfolio logic, and ruthless promotion before a single Chinese city is named.
- **Teach the mechanism through successive cases, then name the scorecard.** Huawei, BYD, DJI, Geely, Vivo establish "Beijing played no role in their creation." Hefei, Wuhu, Ningde show cities acting as equity holders. Only then does the essay open the cadre evaluation file: GDP points, age ceilings, revenue retention. Cases first, system second—so the incentive model feels discovered, not asserted.
- **Concede the system's failure modes with a boundary.** Overcapacity in autos, negative real deposit rates that force savings into firms, fudged GDP numbers from promotion-hungry mayors, and corruption that greases deals are all on the page. Naming them does not retreat from the claim; it shows the authors know where the tournament overextends, which makes the remaining claim harder to dismiss as boosterism.
- **Map the foreign case onto the reader's own history.** The closing section ("The Chicago way") finds Richard J. Daley, Robert Moses, and William Hartsfield running the same machine in mid-century America. The surprise is not that China invented something alien; it is that the West abandoned something it once had. The claim ends at its true size: revive the old model, do not import a new one.

## Writing moves

- **Opening:** a one-line counterfactual the reader can inhabit (YC runs San Francisco), then the thesis as a near-slogan ("continent-sized Silicon Valley"). No abstract definition of industrial policy first.
- **Structure:** frame swap → firm cases that exclude Beijing → city VC portfolios → the incentive scorecard (Li Qiang's climb as one life) → how clusters are built (Tesla Shanghai, Kunshan) → the capital fuel and Beijing's cleanup role → perverse outcomes → the American/European historical rhyme → the flipped lesson. One mechanism, climbed once.
- **Transitions:** short declaratives that place the reader ("Local heroes"; "Growth is the best policy"; "The Chicago way"). Headings name the next demand on the argument rather than topic labels.
- **Rhythm:** long evidentiary paragraphs broken by short claim sentences ("Beijing set a prize, and local bureaucrats competed to win it."). Numbers do the work of adjectives (81 percent stake, 30 percent IRR, 330 GWh, 4.1 million manufacturing enterprises).
- **Concrete examples:** named firms, named mayors, named land deals, exact promotion points from a Shaoxing scorecard. The reader never meets "industrial policy" without a city that spent almost a tenth of its revenue on one LCD line.
- **What they cut:** no general history of Deng's reforms as a preamble, no comparative theory literature review, no how-to checklist for Western mayors. The American parallel arrives only after the Chinese mechanism is fully built, so it lands as recognition rather than throat-clearing.
- **Ending:** two sentences that refuse the fashionable conclusion. Not a new economic model. The old one, restored.

## Excerpts

1. Frame as slogan: "China is not the world's largest command economy. It is a continent-sized Silicon Valley."
2. Opening analogy: "Imagine if Y Combinator owned and ran San Francisco."
3. The renamed role: "But Beijing's role is more akin to being a rulemaker in a great tournament."
4. Cases that exclude the center: "What all have in common is that Beijing played no role in their creation. They were private companies encouraged by local governments."
5. Closing flip: "The lesson from China's economic miracle is not that the West needs a new economic model. It needs its old one."

## Exercise

Pick one policy success people in your field usually credit to a central actor (a CEO, a ministry, a platform). Write the popular story in two sentences. Then list three local or subordinate actors who actually competed for the prize, what each captured if they won, and what scorecard promoted or fired them. Rewrite the core claim so the tournament, not the center, is the subject. End with one sentence that says whether your own institution still runs that tournament—or abandoned it—and the boundary where central rulemaking remains necessary.
