# Putting Ideas into Words — Paul Graham

- Link: https://paulgraham.com/words.html
- Author: Paul Graham (@paulg), February 2022
- Genre: entrepreneurial essay (on thinking and writing)
- Studied: 2026-10-11

## Core claim

Writing is not how you record ideas you already have; it is the test that makes them precise and complete, so anyone who has not written about a topic does not yet have fully formed ideas about it.

## Thinking moves

- **Start from a felt experience, then push it to its consequence.** The first two-thirds establish something most writers already know (writing changes your ideas). The payoff is the step nobody takes: if that is always true, then people who never write have no fully formed ideas about anything nontrivial. The conviction is earned because the shocking claim is just the obvious claim followed one step further.
- **Name the convention he is breaking.** He states the polite fiction ("whatever you wrote was what you thought before you wrote it") so the reader sees it is false before he argues anything.
- **Pre-empt the strongest objection with a boundary, not a retreat.** Chess and math look like counterexamples; he concedes them and redraws the line at ideas you can express in a formal language. Architecture gets the same treatment: other ways of exploring are fine, writing still teaches you more. Each concession makes the remaining claim harder to attack.
- **Use himself as the evidence.** Lisp hacking and startups, two subjects he knows well, still taught him things when he wrote about them. Two weeks per essay and 50 rereads make the standard concrete.
- **Size the claim precisely at the end.** Writing is necessary, not sufficient. He refuses the overclaim, which is why the strong claim survives.

## Writing moves

- **Opening:** the first sentence is the thesis in its mildest form, stated as experience the reader shares. No preamble, no anecdote.
- **Structure:** an escalating ladder. Uncontroversial premise, then the mechanism (the neutral stranger), then objections and boundaries, then an explicit pivot ("leads to another that many people will find shocking"), then the conclusion.
- **Transitions:** he tells the reader where they are in the argument ("This much, I assume, won't be that controversial"; "The reason I've spent so long establishing this rather obvious point"). Signposting replaces headings.
- **Sentence rhythm:** mostly plain declaratives, broken by very short ones that land a point ("Happy now?"; "At best it's a plan for a plan.").
- **Concrete images:** the head-tapping movie trope ("It's all up here") makes vague plans vivid in one line. The neutral stranger turns the advice to edit your draft into a reader with demands you can satisfy.
- **What he cut:** no history of writing, no definitions, no list of tips. About 1,050 words in the body. Footnotes hold asides (Seneca's letters, the Palo Alto walk) so the main line stays clean.
- **Ending:** a two-sentence close that limits the claim instead of inflating it. Necessary, not sufficient.

## Excerpts

1. Opening as shared experience: "Writing about something, even something you know well, usually shows you that you didn't know it as well as you thought."
2. Naming the convention: "Once you publish something, the convention is that whatever you wrote was what you thought before you wrote it."
3. The mechanism as a person: "You have to pretend to be a neutral reader who knows nothing of what's in your head, only what you wrote."
4. Escalation, signposted: "The reason I've spent so long establishing this rather obvious point is that it leads to another that many people will find shocking."
5. The calibrated ending: "But though it's not a sufficient condition, it is a necessary one."

## Exercise

Take one idea you believe you understand. Write it in 200 words for a neutral stranger, then reread it as that stranger and list every missing point or unqualified sentence they would object to. Fix each one. Then ask: if this is true, what follows one step further that would surprise people? Write that sentence, and the boundary where it stops being true.
