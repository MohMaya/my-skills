# Everything rhymes with porcelain — Charles C. Mann

- Author: Charles C. Mann (@CharlesCMann), Works in Progress, October 14, 2026
- Genre: science explainer (materials science told through history)
- Found via: @s8mb (Sam Bowman, WiP editor-in-chief) on Oct 15, 2026 called Mann "one of my favourite writers" and recommended this debut piece (about 221 likes, 55 bookmarks, ~14K impressions); @WorksInProgMag and @RuxandraTeslo shared it the same week
- Studied: 2026-10-18

## Core claim

Dekton, the costly surface his new induction cooktop required, is basically porcelain, and porcelain is at once one of humanity's oldest high technologies and one of its newest, so its 3,000-year story shows materials science quietly remaking the physical world while attention goes to the digital one.

## Thinking moves

- **Let an everyday irritation pose the question.** The piece starts as a consumer complaint: an induction cooktop sold without its surface, and a surface that costs five times tempered glass. The question "What on earth is Dekton?" is one the reader has asked of some object. Materials science arrives as the answer to it.
- **Give the question two true answers; the gap is the thesis.** Dekton is a 2013 invention that "would have been almost impossible to fashion even thirty years ago." It is also "basically porcelain." Both are true. He states the contradiction outright as the claim: oldest and newest high technology at once.
- **Rhyme the unfamiliar case with one the reader knows.** Iron had its second flowering with the Bessemer and Siemens-Martin processes; porcelain is having its own now. Renaissance china sets become "the private jets and invite-only clubs of the Renaissance era." Each rhyme gives the distant case a shape the reader already holds.
- **Grant the default its due, then point at what it hides.** Attention on AI is "rightfully" focused, he says, before arguing that it obscures the transformation of the built world. The essay does not argue against AI. It argues about where attention misses.
- **Explain the failure by the missing fact.** Europe failed for centuries not from lack of money or effort but because nobody knew porcelain was made from kaolin, with China stone added for enough glassy silicate. The chemistry section comes first, so the failure reads as the mechanism's proof.
- **Bound the claim, then price it in people.** "Most of its innovations have been incremental," he concedes, and keeps the claim about the accumulated total. Then he prices the incremental: his mother-in-law's fall on a wet tile, his daughter's porcelain hip, and one back-of-the-envelope comparison, tucked in parentheses behind a question.

## Writing moves

- **Opening:** one long, self-interrupting sentence ("did I mention it was pricey?") that acts out the irritation, then the question in quotation marks. Voice first; the thesis arrives in the second paragraph.
- **Structure:** the question, then two answers, then the stated paradox, then why it matters (AI attention hides materials science). Then three eras under one-word headings that each name a substance: Kaolin (how it works), Alltinktur (why Europe failed and how Meissen finally succeeded), Dekton (Porcelain 3.0 and the materials-science wave). Then two family cases and a close across generations.
- **Transitions:** versioning carries the reader across three centuries in one sentence ("If Meissen was Porcelain 2.0..."). Stage directions move the cast ("Enter Count Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus"). "To give a personal example" and "Another personal example" turn from history to stakes.
- **Rhythm:** long, clause-stacked paragraphs broken by short deadpan lines: "Surprisingly, he was unable to deliver." "And so on." "He did not live to enjoy his triumph." The deadpan gives the history its comic timing.
- **Concrete examples:** $30 per square foot against five times that; 1,200 degrees centigrade; 25,000 metric tons of pressure; six years and almost $200 million; lenses that focus sunlight on only 10 to 15 square centimeters. The chemistry is one formula, unpacked in a parenthesis, and one picture: clay crystals stacked like playing cards in a deck.
- **What he cut:** no survey of materials science, no second material, no tour of applications. The remaining innovations (nanotech pore filling, silver-ion layers, lithium disilicate implants) get one "I could go on" sentence and stop. Asides (fireplaces, the formula, where the scaffold goes) live in parentheses so the main line keeps moving.
- **Ending:** back to the kitchen across three generations: his grandmother's Meissen on the sideboard, his own pots dropped on a porcelain slab, his son's blank look. The last line proves the early claim that the change is "so ubiquitous as to be almost invisible" without restating it.

## Excerpts

1. The question: "‘What on earth is Dekton?’ I asked the heavens, or at least my wife."
2. The second answer: "The other answer, equally true, is that Dekton is basically porcelain."
3. The paradox as claim: "Paradoxically, porcelain is both one of humankind’s oldest high technologies and one of its newest."
4. Versioning as transition: "If Meissen was Porcelain 2.0, the Dekton for my cooktop is Porcelain 3.0 or more."
5. The close: "He had never lived in a world that wasn’t made of porcelain."

## Exercise

Pick an ordinary object you used this week whose price or behavior surprised you. Write the question it raises in one line. Give two true answers: one that makes the thing new and one that makes it old, and state the gap between them as your claim. Explain the mechanism with one household picture and at most one formula. Tell the history through the fact whose absence kept people from making it. Rhyme it with one technology the reader already knows had a second flowering. Close on a person who cannot see the change because they have never lived without it, with no summary.
