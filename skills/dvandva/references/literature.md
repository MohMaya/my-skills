# Dvandva: the literature behind the moves

Each source below earns its place by feeding one move; headings follow the moves in `../SKILL.md` (moves 7 and 8, Lessons and Produce, need no source). Quotes are verbatim from the cited source; paraphrases are marked as such. Attributions known to be apocryphal say so.

## 1. Understand: you do not know it until you can say it

- **Socratic elenchus and aporia.** Socrates questions an interlocutor until his confident definition collapses into contradiction and he reaches aporia, the honest state of not knowing. In Plato's *Meno* (79e-80b) Meno compares Socrates to the torpedo fish that numbs whoever touches it; the slave-boy passage (84a-c) argues that being brought to perplexity is progress, because now he wants to find out. Move: find what you do not understand before you claim it. Source: Plato, *Meno*, trans. G.M.A. Grube (Hackett, 1976).
- **Paul Graham, writing as thinking.** "Writing about something, even something you know well, usually shows you that you didn't know it as well as you thought." And: "If writing down your ideas always makes them more precise and more complete, then no one who hasn't written about a topic has fully formed ideas about it." He also names the empathy test: "You have to pretend to be a neutral reader who knows nothing of what's in your head, only what you wrote." Source: Paul Graham, "Putting Ideas into Words" (Feb 2022), https://paulgraham.com/words.html.
- **Feynman's freshman test.** David Goodstein asked Feynman why spin-1/2 particles obey Fermi-Dirac statistics. Feynman offered a freshman lecture, then returned: "You know, I couldn't do it. I couldn't reduce it to the freshman level. That means we really don't understand it." Source: D. Goodstein, "Richard P. Feynman, Teacher", *Physics Today* 42(2), Feb 1989; reprinted in D. & J. Goodstein, *Feynman's Lost Lecture* (1996), p. 52; quoted at https://magazine.caltech.edu/post/feynman-at-100. The popular line "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough" is unsourced for both Feynman and Einstein.
- **Amazon's narrative memo.** Bezos's 9 June 2004 email to senior staff replaced PowerPoint with narrative memos, arguing (paraphrase) that full sentences force the writer to show what matters more and how ideas connect, which bullets let you skip. Amazon later standardised on six-page narratives read silently at the start of the meeting. Sources: Business Insider reprint of the email (2015), https://www.businessinsider.com/jeff-bezos-email-against-powerpoint-presentations-2015-7; Bezos, 2017 Letter to Shareholders (on great memos taking a week or more to write).
- **Rumelt's kernel.** A good strategy has a kernel of three parts: a diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. Bad strategy is fluff, a failure to face the problem, or goals mistaken for strategy. Move: the crux names the real problem, not an aspiration. Source: Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (Crown, 2011), ch. 5.

## 2. Purvapaksha: the opponent's case at its strongest

- **Purvapaksha-siddhanta.** Classical Indian commentary (most formally Mimamsa and Vedanta) states the prior view (purvapaksha) fully, often more forcefully than its holders, before the established conclusion (siddhanta) answers it. Move: you earn your answer only after stating the rival well. Source: standard method in Shabara's *Mimamsa-sutra-bhashya* and Shankara's *Brahma-sutra-bhashya*; see the overview in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Mimamsa" and "Indian Philosophy of Language".
- **Rapoport's rules (steelmanning).** Dennett's version: first re-express your target's position "so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way.'" Then list points of agreement, then anything you learned, and only then rebut. Source: Daniel Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (Norton, 2013), ch. 3, after Anatol Rapoport.
- **Tetlock's superforecasters and active open-mindedness.** The best forecasters treat beliefs as hypotheses to test, seek out disconfirming views, and update in small steps; actively open-minded thinking (Baron's term) was among the strongest predictors of accuracy. Source: Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting* (Crown, 2015); Jonathan Baron, *Thinking and Deciding* (4th ed., 2008).

## 3. Dvandva: let the opposites fight

- **Dvandva in the Bhagavad Gita.** Dvandva is the pair of opposites (pleasure-pain, gain-loss, victory-defeat). Gita 2.38: "Treat pleasure and pain, gain and loss, and victory and defeat as equal; then engage in battle" (translations vary); 2.45 urges Arjuna to be *nirdvandva*, free from the pairs. The setting is itself an inner conflict: Arjuna collapses in doubt on the battlefield (ch. 1) and acts only after he has argued it through. Move: wrestle the opposites, then choose with equanimity, not with attachment to the side you started on. Source: Gita 2.38, 2.45, IIT Kanpur Gita Supersite, https://www.gitasupersite.iitk.ac.in.
- **Nyaya debate: vada, jalpa, vitanda.** *Nyaya Sutra* 1.2.1 defines vada as discussion in which thesis and counter-thesis are each supported and refuted by valid means of knowledge and reasoning; 1.2.2 jalpa as the same plus quibbles, futile rejoinders, and clinchers, aiming at victory; 1.2.3 vitanda as jalpa that establishes no position of its own, pure demolition. Commentators treat vada as the debate for truth (classically between teacher and student). Move: the inner fight is vada. Source: Gautama, *Nyaya Sutra* with Vatsyayana's *Bhashya*, trans. Ganganatha Jha, https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/nyaya-sutras-of-gautama-with-commentaries/d/doc1907062.html.
- **Montaigne's essais.** *Essai* means a trial or attempt; Montaigne used the form to put his own judgment to the test, and his motto was "Que sais-je?" (What do I know?). Source: Michel de Montaigne, *Essais* (1580-1595), esp. "Of Experience" (III.13); trans. Donald Frame (Stanford, 1958).
- **Munger's inversion.** Munger often quoted the mathematician Jacobi, "Invert, always invert", and gave a 1986 commencement talk on how to guarantee a miserable life as a way of finding a good one. Move: ask what would make this fail or embarrass. Source: Charlie Munger, Harvard School commencement, 13 June 1986, in *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (2005).
- **Premortem.** Assume the project has already failed and write the reasons it did. Klein cites Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989): imagining the outcome has already happened ("prospective hindsight") raised the number of reasons people generated by about 30%. Note: the 1989 study counted reasons, not their accuracy, though Klein describes it as better identification. Source: Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", *Harvard Business Review*, Sept 2007, https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem.
- **Red-teaming.** An independent team plays the adversary to expose a plan's blind spots; it works only when the red team is truly independent and the owner wants the bad news. Source: Micah Zenko, *Red Team: How to Succeed by Thinking Like the Enemy* (Basic Books, 2015).
- **Kahneman's System 2.** System 1 is fast and fluent; System 2 is slow, effortful, and lazy, and it endorses System 1's answers unless forced to check. A draft that came easily is System 1 until audited. Source: Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011).

## 4. Stitch: keep what was true on each side

- **Dialectic.** Thesis meets antithesis; what survives is a higher synthesis that keeps what was true in each. The triad is usually credited to Hegel, but it is Fichte's vocabulary and Hegel rarely used it; Hegel's own term is *Aufheben* (to cancel and preserve at once). Source: G.E. Mueller, "The Hegel Legend of 'Thesis-Antithesis-Synthesis'", *Journal of the History of Ideas* 19(3), 1958.

## 5. Distill: perfection by removal

- **Pascal's longer letter.** "Je n'ai fait celle-ci plus longue que parce que je n'ai pas eu le loisir de la faire plus courte." (I have made this one longer only because I did not have the leisure to make it shorter.) Source: Blaise Pascal, *Lettres provinciales*, Letter XVI (1657); verified at https://quoteinvestigator.com/2012/04/28/shorter-letter/.
- **Saint-Exupery.** "Il semble que la perfection soit atteinte non quand il n'y a plus rien à ajouter, mais quand il n'y a plus rien à retrancher." (Perfection is reached not when there is nothing left to add, but when there is nothing left to take away.) Source: Antoine de Saint-Exupery, *Terre des hommes* (1939), ch. 3, "L'Avion".
- **Michelangelo and David: apocryphal.** Quote Investigator finds "no substantive evidence that Michelangelo or any other great sculptor made this remark." The idea appears unattributed in 1858, then as a joke credited to Ruskin, a rustic, and others; the Michelangelo-and-David version shows up in print in the 1960s-70s. Use it as an image, never as his words. Michelangelo's real thinking on carving by removal ("per forza di levare") is in his letter to Benedetto Varchi (1549) and his sonnet "Non ha l'ottimo artista alcun concetto" (c. 1538-44): the best artist has no idea that a single block of marble does not already contain. Source: https://quoteinvestigator.com/2014/06/22/chip-away/; *The Poetry of Michelangelo*, trans. James Saslow (Yale, 1991), no. 151.

## 6. Empathy: model the reader

- **The curse of knowledge.** Once you know something, you struggle to imagine not knowing it. Better-informed traders failed to ignore their private information when predicting what less-informed people would do. Source: C. Camerer, G. Loewenstein & M. Weber, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings", *Journal of Political Economy* 97(5), 1989.
- **The tappers.** People tapping well-known songs predicted listeners would name about half; listeners named about 1 in 40 (2.5%). Source: Elizabeth Newton, Stanford PhD dissertation (1990), as reported in Chip & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007), and Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), ch. 3, which calls the curse of knowledge the main cause of bad writing.
- **Graham's stranger** (above) is the operational form: read it as a stranger who knows only the page.

## What the literature converges on

Understanding is proven by explanation, not felt. Judgment improves by stating the rival at its best and attacking your own case before anyone else does, as a debate for truth rather than victory. What survives must then be cut until nothing is left to remove, and read from the seat of a reader who lacks your context. That is the seven moves in `../SKILL.md`.
